    Travertine Sealing 101: Complete Guide for Arizona Homeowners

    📅 July 2025 ⏱ 7 min read ✍️ Go Go Gloss

    Travertine is the defining surface of Scottsdale outdoor living. You'll find it on pool decks, patios, driveways, and walkways across the Valley — and for good reason. Its natural texture, warm earth tones, and relatively cool surface temperature make it ideal for Arizona's climate. But travertine is also one of the most misunderstood paver surfaces when it comes to sealing. Use the wrong product or technique and you can ruin the appearance of an expensive installation in one afternoon. This guide covers everything Arizona homeowners need to know.

    Why Travertine Is Different From Other Pavers

    Unlike manufactured concrete pavers or kiln-fired brick, travertine is a natural limestone formed by mineral springs. Its defining characteristic — and greatest challenge — is porosity. Travertine is full of natural voids, pits, and channels that formed during its creation. This open structure gives it a beautiful, organic appearance, but it also means the stone absorbs moisture, stains, and cleaning chemicals readily.

    In Arizona's environment, this porosity creates specific risks. Desert dust and mineral deposits from Phoenix's notoriously hard water can penetrate unsealed travertine and discolor it permanently. Pool chemical splashback — chlorine and muriatic acid — can etch unsealed travertine surfaces. And Arizona's intense UV radiation fades the natural warm tones of unprotected stone over time, leaving it looking bleached and aged.

    Proper sealing preserves the stone's natural beauty and prevents these forms of damage. But travertine requires different sealer chemistry than concrete or brick — applying the wrong product produces results that range from disappointing to genuinely damaging.

    Types of Travertine Finish — and How They Affect Sealing

    The way your travertine was cut and finished determines its porosity level and which sealing approach is appropriate.

    Honed

    Honed travertine has a smooth, matte surface produced by grinding and polishing. It's the most common finish for Scottsdale patios and pool decks — elegant without being slippery, and relatively easy to clean. Honed travertine is moderately porous and accepts penetrating sealers well. Its natural pits may be filled with grout or color-matched filler, or left open depending on the homeowner's preference.

    Tumbled

    Tumbled travertine has been processed to produce rough, rounded edges and an antique, weathered appearance. This finish is popular for rustic or Mediterranean-style outdoor spaces. Tumbled travertine is more porous than honed — its open surface texture absorbs sealer faster and requires more product per square foot. It also absorbs UV damage more quickly, meaning more frequent resealing.

    Brushed

    Brushed travertine falls between honed and tumbled — it has a slightly textured surface achieved by wire brushing, giving it grip without the full roughness of tumbled stone. Porosity is moderate, and sealing requirements are similar to honed travertine.

    The Right Sealer for Travertine

    This is where most DIY and even some contractor mistakes happen. Travertine requires a penetrating (impregnating) sealer — a product that soaks into the stone rather than forming a film on the surface.

    Penetrating sealers work by filling the stone's pores with a hydrophobic and oleophobic resin. The stone looks essentially the same as unsealed, but liquids and stains bead on the surface instead of soaking in. The sealer is invisible, breathable, and does not change the stone's natural texture or appearance. This is the correct approach for travertine.

    What you should not use on travertine:

    • Film-forming acrylic sealers: These create a surface coating rather than penetrating the stone. On travertine, this film can peel and flake as the porous stone's natural moisture movement breaks the bond. It can also create a plastic-looking sheen that looks unnatural on natural stone.
    • High-gloss wet-look sealers: These are film-forming products that create a shiny, enhanced appearance. On pool deck travertine, they create a dangerous slip hazard. On patios, they look artificial and require stripping to remove when they fail.
    • Water-based sealers with low solids content: Consumer-grade water-based products don't penetrate travertine effectively and provide minimal protection in Arizona's UV environment.

    Anti-Slip Considerations for Pool Decks

    Travertine pool decks require special attention to slip resistance. Travertine is naturally more slip-resistant when dry than smooth concrete, but when wet — which is constantly the case around a pool — any sealer application changes this equation.

    Penetrating sealers generally maintain travertine's natural texture and slip resistance better than film-forming products. However, even a penetrating sealer can slightly increase slip risk on wet travertine, particularly on honed surfaces. For pool decks, anti-slip additives — fine silica or polymer particles that create microscopic texture — should be incorporated into any sealer application.

    A matte or natural finish is strongly recommended for pool deck travertine. Never apply a high-gloss or wet-look product to a pool deck without anti-slip additives, and even then, a natural-finish penetrating sealer is the safer and more appropriate choice.

    How Often to Seal Travertine in Arizona

    Travertine requires more frequent sealing than concrete pavers because penetrating sealers, while durable, don't provide the thick protective film of a surface sealer. Arizona's UV intensity accelerates sealer degradation in the pores of the stone.

    • Honed travertine: Every 2–3 years. The smoother surface has slightly lower penetrating sealer consumption and UV exposure per unit area.
    • Tumbled travertine: Every 1.5–2 years. Higher surface porosity means more sealer exposure and faster depletion. Pool deck tumbled travertine may need attention at the 18-month mark.

    The water bead test is your reliable indicator: pour a small amount of water onto the surface. If it beads up, you have protection. If it soaks in within 30 seconds, it's time to reseal.

    Filling Travertine Holes Before Sealing

    Travertine's natural pits and voids are a characteristic feature of the stone, but they also collect dirt, grime, and algae over time. Whether to fill them before sealing is a personal preference, but there are practical considerations on each side.

    Filling the pits (called "grouting" the travertine) gives the surface a more uniform appearance and makes cleaning easier — no recesses for debris to accumulate. For honed travertine in high-use areas, filling is generally recommended. Professionals use color-matched epoxy filler or cementitious grout that blends with the stone's natural tones.

    Leaving the pits open preserves travertine's natural, organic character — a look many homeowners prefer, particularly for tumbled stone where the rough, ancient aesthetic is the point. Unsealed pits are harder to keep clean but are entirely natural.

    If your travertine has existing grout fill that has cracked or discolored, addressing this before sealing produces much better results. Sealing over cracked filler locks in the problem.

    Cost of Travertine Sealing in Scottsdale

    Travertine sealing costs more than comparable concrete paver jobs due to the specialty products required and the more careful application technique involved. In the Scottsdale market, expect:

    • $0.50–$0.65/sq ft for standard travertine patios and driveways in good condition
    • $0.65–$0.85/sq ft for pool decks requiring anti-slip treatment, heavily porous tumbled travertine, or jobs requiring pre-sealing hole filling
    • Additional $100–$250 for efflorescence treatment if white salt deposits are present (very common in Phoenix-area travertine due to hard water)

    For a typical 800 sq ft travertine pool deck and patio combination in Scottsdale, budget $500–$700 for a complete professional job with quality penetrating sealer. That's a reasonable investment to protect a natural stone surface that costs $8–$15 per sq ft to install.
