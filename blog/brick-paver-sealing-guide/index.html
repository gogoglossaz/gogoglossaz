    Brick Paver Sealing: Complete Guide for Phoenix Homeowners

    📅 July 2025 ⏱ 5 min read ✍️ Go Go Gloss

    Brick pavers bring a classic, timeless look to Phoenix driveways, walkways, and patios. Whether you have traditional clay brick, modern concrete brick pavers, or reclaimed antique brick, each type has specific sealing requirements — and skipping or shortcutting the process in Arizona's harsh environment leads to rapid deterioration. This guide covers everything you need to know about protecting brick pavers in the Phoenix metro.

    Types of Brick Pavers Found in Phoenix

    Not all brick is the same, and the differences matter significantly when it comes to sealing product selection and technique.

    • Clay brick pavers: Traditional fired clay bricks are the classic choice. They're highly porous, absorb moisture and staining agents readily, and the iron oxide pigments that give them their red and brown tones are vulnerable to fading under Arizona's intense UV. Older Phoenix neighborhoods frequently have clay brick driveways and walkways that have never been sealed — and they show it.
    • Concrete brick pavers: More common in newer builds throughout the Phoenix metro, concrete brick pavers are manufactured to mimic the look of clay brick while offering more consistent sizing and color. They're still porous but somewhat less so than traditional clay. Most new construction in Scottsdale, Chandler, and Gilbert uses concrete brick or concrete pavers rather than clay.
    • Reclaimed/antique brick: Some homeowners use reclaimed brick for its character and aged appearance. These bricks have often been weathered and may have residue from previous mortar, paint, or sealers. They require a gentle approach — both in cleaning and sealing — to preserve their character without causing damage.

    Why Brick Needs Sealing in Arizona

    Brick's natural porosity makes it particularly vulnerable to the specific conditions Phoenix homeowners deal with:

    • Stain absorption: Unsealed brick acts like a sponge for oil drips from vehicles, rust from metal furniture or equipment, and organic staining from leaves and landscaping. Once absorbed into porous brick, many stains are impossible to remove without aggressive chemical treatment that can damage the brick itself.
    • UV color fading: The iron oxide that creates brick's characteristic red and warm brown tones breaks down under sustained UV exposure. Phoenix averages over 300 sunny days per year with UV index values among the highest in the country. Unsealed clay brick can fade noticeably within 2–3 years, losing the rich color that made it attractive in the first place.
    • Joint sand loss in monsoon season: Arizona's summer monsoon storms deliver intense, short rainfall events — sometimes an inch of rain in under an hour. This kind of downpour washes joint sand out of unsealed brick installations quickly. Without joint sand, pavers shift, weeds establish in the gaps, and the structural integrity of the installation deteriorates.
    • Efflorescence: White or gray salt deposits (efflorescence) are extremely common on Phoenix-area brick due to the Valley's notoriously hard water and alkaline soil. These mineral salts migrate through the brick as water evaporates, leaving chalky deposits on the surface. Left untreated and then sealed over, efflorescence becomes permanently locked in.

    Treating Efflorescence Before Sealing

    This step is non-negotiable for Phoenix brick sealing jobs. Efflorescence must be fully removed before any sealer is applied — sealing over it locks the mineral deposits in place permanently and often causes the sealer to fail in those areas within months.

    Effective efflorescence removal:

    1. Dry brush first: Use a stiff bristle brush to remove loose surface deposits. Some light efflorescence will come off with brushing alone.
    2. Apply efflorescence cleaner or diluted muriatic acid: For stubborn deposits, a diluted muriatic acid solution (typically 10:1 water to acid) or a dedicated efflorescence cleaner applied to a wet surface will dissolve the mineral salts. Let it dwell for 5–10 minutes — you'll see fizzing as the acid reacts with the alkaline deposits.
    3. Scrub and rinse thoroughly: Scrub with a stiff brush and rinse with large amounts of water. Acid treatments must be neutralized completely before sealing — residual acid will compromise sealer adhesion.
    4. Neutralize: Apply a baking soda and water solution to neutralize any remaining acid, then rinse again.
    5. Allow complete drying: Brick must be fully dry before sealing — 48–72 hours in Arizona summer heat, potentially longer in cooler months. Even slightly damp brick will cause adhesion problems and sealer whitening.

    Best Sealer Types for Brick

    Brick sealer selection depends on the type of brick and the desired aesthetic outcome:

    • Penetrating/impregnating sealers: The best choice for preservation-focused applications. They protect against moisture and staining without changing the brick's appearance. Ideal for clay brick where natural appearance should be maintained, and for any application where slip resistance is a concern.
    • Film-forming sealers for color enhancement: Acrylic or polyurethane film-forming sealers can deepen and enrich the color of darker concrete brick pavers significantly, producing the "wet look" that many homeowners prefer on driveways and front entries. Work well on concrete brick; use cautiously on clay brick.
    • Avoid high-gloss on authentic clay brick: High-gloss wet look products on traditional clay brick look unnatural — the plastic-like sheen clashes with the organic, rustic character of the material. If enhancement is desired, a semi-gloss or satin product produces more realistic results. Most professionals recommend matte or low-sheen options for clay.

    How to Prepare Brick Pavers for Sealing

    Preparation is the most time-consuming part of a quality brick sealing job, and it's where shortcuts have the biggest negative impact on results.

    1. Power wash at 2,500–3,000 PSI: Brick is softer than concrete and requires lower pressure than a concrete paver cleaning — excessive pressure can erode the brick surface and blow out joint sand. Use a wide-angle tip (25–40 degrees) rather than a pinpoint nozzle.
    2. Treat efflorescence as described above if present.
    3. Allow complete drying: 48–72 hours minimum in Arizona summer; longer in cooler or humid weather. This is the step most often rushed, and rushed drying is among the top causes of sealer failure.
    4. Re-sand joints with polymeric sand: Inspect the joints and top off or replace joint sand with polymeric sand. Polymeric sand activates with water to form a semi-rigid bond that resists washing out and resists weed germination significantly better than regular jointing sand. This step, combined with sealing, dramatically extends the time before weed intrusion becomes an issue.
    5. Activate and cure polymeric sand: After re-sanding, lightly mist the surface to activate the polymer binders. Allow the sand to cure per manufacturer instructions — typically 24 hours — before applying sealer.

    How Often to Seal Brick in Phoenix

    Resealing frequency depends on the brick type, sealer used, and how heavily the area is used:

    • Clay brick pavers: Every 2–3 years in Phoenix. Clay's high porosity means sealer depletes faster than on less porous surfaces. High-traffic areas like driveways may need attention at the 2-year mark; lightly used walkways may go 3 years.
    • Concrete brick pavers: Every 3 years for most applications. Concrete brick is more consistent in density and slightly less porous than clay, so sealer lasts longer. Film-forming sealers on concrete brick may show surface wear sooner than penetrating sealers and should be inspected annually.

    As with all paver types, the water bead test is your most reliable guide: apply a small amount of water to the surface. Beading means protection is intact. Rapid absorption means it's time to reseal. Scheduling resealing before the sealer fully fails — rather than waiting until the pavers are completely bare — produces better adhesion and a more durable result.
