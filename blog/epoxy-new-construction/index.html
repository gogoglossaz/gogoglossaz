    Epoxy Floors for New Construction: Start Right

    📅 July 2025 ⏱ 4 min read ✍️ Go Go Gloss

    If you're building a new home, adding a shop, or constructing a commercial space in the Phoenix metro area, you have a window of opportunity that retrofit projects never get: a clean slate. Fresh concrete with no stains, no old sealers, no previous coating failures, and no contamination from years of use. New construction is the ideal time to install epoxy floors — and with the right planning, it's also the easiest and most cost-effective time to do it.

    Why New Construction Is the Best Time for Epoxy

    Homeowners and builders who defer the epoxy decision often end up paying more later. Here's what makes new construction the preferred scenario for any epoxy installer:

    • No existing coatings to strip. Stripping old paint, sealers, or failed coatings from a concrete slab requires additional labor, equipment, and time. In new construction, that work simply doesn't exist. The diamond grinder goes directly to bare, clean concrete.
    • The slab is in its best possible condition. No oil stains from years of vehicle use, no rust rings from cans and equipment, no penetrating contamination that requires deep grinding to remove. A fresh slab is as close to ideal substrate conditions as you'll ever have.
    • Concrete mix and finish can be specified for epoxy. If you're involved in the design phase of a new building, you can specify concrete mix, finishing method, and surface texture that optimizes for epoxy adhesion. This is an option that simply doesn't exist on an existing slab.
    • Construction sequencing can be planned around the floor. On a retrofit project, the floor has to work around everything else. In new construction, the epoxy installation can be sequenced properly within the construction schedule — after the slab is clean and other trades are done, before final occupancy.

    Concrete Cure Time Requirement

    The most important new construction scheduling consideration is concrete cure time. This is non-negotiable: concrete must be allowed to cure adequately before epoxy is applied, or the installation will fail.

    • Minimum 28 days of cure time. Concrete reaches approximately 80% of its design strength at 28 days under normal curing conditions. Epoxy applied before this point is bonding to concrete that is still off-gassing moisture — a primary cause of epoxy delamination.
    • Moisture content below 4 lbs/1,000 sq ft/24 hrs. This is the standard moisture vapor emission rate (MVER) threshold for epoxy applications. Your epoxy contractor should test the slab moisture before application using a calcium chloride test or an in-situ relative humidity probe. Arizona's dry climate is generally helpful here, but a fresh slab is still actively drying internally even when the surface feels dry.
    • Don't let other trades contaminate the slab. Construction sites are busy environments. Once the slab is poured and curing, protect it from oil drips from equipment, concrete sealer applications by other trades, paint overspray, and drywall compound or stucco splatter. Any of these contaminate the surface and require additional preparation before epoxy can be applied.
    • Mark the slab in the construction schedule. Put "HOLD FOR EPOXY — DO NOT COAT OR SEAL" in writing on the construction schedule and communicate it to the GC and all subcontractors. Sealers and curing compounds applied to concrete by other trades can prevent epoxy adhesion and may require extensive mechanical removal.

    Coordinating with Your General Contractor

    Epoxy is a finish trade — it goes near the end of construction, after the messy work is done. Understanding where it fits in the construction sequence prevents both delays and damage to the finished floor.

    • Trades that must be complete before epoxy: rough and finish mechanical (HVAC, plumbing), electrical (conduit penetrations through the slab), framing and drywall, painting (avoid overspray on the slab). Any work that generates dust, moisture, or debris should be done and cleaned up before epoxy goes down.
    • Trades that follow epoxy: final cleaning, punch list work that doesn't involve the floor, occupancy inspection. Once the epoxy is down, the floor is done — protect it from construction traffic with ram board or similar protection if other trades still need access.
    • Get a written hold period in the GC schedule. Verbal agreements in construction are forgotten quickly. A written schedule hold for the epoxy installation — with dates and sequencing documented — protects your installation from being rushed, pushed back, or exposed to contamination from other work happening concurrently.

    New Construction Epoxy Specifications

    When including epoxy in a new construction scope, the following specifications should be written into the contract — not left to the installer's discretion:

    • Diamond grind, not acid etch. Diamond grinding opens the concrete surface profile mechanically, without the chemical variability and neutralization requirements of acid etching. On a new slab, diamond grinding is cleaner, more consistent, and produces better adhesion. If a contractor proposes acid etching as the only surface prep method, ask why.
    • 100% solids epoxy base, not water-based. Water-based epoxy products are easier to apply and have lower VOC, but they don't deliver the same film build, chemical resistance, or adhesion of a 100% solids system. Specify 100% solids in the contract.
    • UV-stable topcoat: polyaspartic or polyurea. For any space with UV exposure — including Arizona garages where sunlight reaches the floor through open doors — a UV-stable aliphatic topcoat prevents yellowing. Specify polyaspartic or aliphatic polyurea, not aromatic formulations.
    • COF test results from the installer. Ask for documented coefficient of friction test results, particularly for any floor that will be used by people (not just vehicle-only areas). This confirms the anti-slip broadcast was applied at the specified rate and the floor meets safety requirements.

    Popular New Construction Finish Choices

    With a fresh slab and no constraints from existing conditions, new construction gives you the full range of epoxy finish options. Here are the most popular choices by application type:

    • Garage: Full-broadcast flake in classic gray or tan blend. Practical, durable, hides tire marks and dirt, and has strong resale appeal. The most popular residential choice in the Phoenix market.
    • Gym or fitness space: Rubber chip broadcast or a solid dark color (charcoal, black). Provides a performance aesthetic and adequate slip resistance for athletic use.
    • Showroom or display space: Metallic epoxy or high-gloss solid color. Creates a premium visual environment. Best in spaces with controlled climate and foot traffic that doesn't include heavy equipment.
    • Commercial warehouse or industrial: Solid industrial color with anti-slip broadcast. Prioritizes durability, chemical resistance, and floor marking compatibility over decorative finish.
