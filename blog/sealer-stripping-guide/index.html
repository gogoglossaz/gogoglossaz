When and How to Strip Old Paver Sealer

📅 July 2025 ⏱ 5 min read ✍️ Go Go Gloss

Why Old Sealer Sometimes Has to Go

Most paver sealing jobs are straightforward: clean the surface, let it dry, apply new sealer. The new coat bonds to the residual old sealer and the paver, extending protection for another 2–3 years. But that process only works when the existing sealer is still in reasonably good condition — adhering firmly to the paver surface and chemically compatible with whatever product is going on top.

When the old sealer has failed, applying new sealer over it does not fix the problem — it magnifies it. The new coat bonds to a surface that is already peeling, hazing, or delaminating. Within months, you have the same failure mode playing out again, now with two layers of bad sealer to deal with instead of one. Stripping becomes the only path to a result that actually lasts.

Signs You Need Stripping (Not Just Resealing)

These are the indicators that tell us a surface needs to be stripped before new sealer goes down:

  • Peeling or flaking patches — If the existing sealer is lifting at the edges, bubbling up in sections, or flaking off when walked on, it has delaminated from the paver surface. New sealer cannot bond to a substrate that is already releasing.
  • White hazing or milky appearance — This is trapped moisture under the old sealer, a condition called blushing. It happens when sealer was applied over wet pavers, or when moisture entered through cracks in an aging seal. The haze cannot be resolved by applying more sealer — the trapped moisture has to come out, which means the sealer has to come off.
  • Dark spotting or watermarks visible under the surface — Dark patches beneath an otherwise intact-looking sealer indicate moisture intrusion or oil contamination that occurred before or during the previous seal job. Sealing over these patches locks the contamination in permanently.
  • Bubbling after a previous seal job — Bubbles that appeared shortly after sealing indicate either moisture off-gassing from below or a solvent compatibility issue. Either way, the layer under the bubbles has failed and must be removed.
  • Incompatible sealer products — Solvent-based sealers applied over water-based (latex) sealers, or vice versa, will not bond correctly. The solvent in some products actually dissolves water-based sealers, creating a gel layer instead of a bond. If you do not know what was previously applied or suspect a product mismatch, stripping is the safe choice.

Chemical Stripping vs Mechanical Removal

Chemical Stripping — Most Common

Chemical stripping is the standard approach for the vast majority of sealer removal jobs. The process uses a chemical stripper formulated for the type of sealer being removed:

  • Xylene-based strippers for solvent-based acrylic sealers — xylene dissolves the acrylic polymer, releasing it from the paver surface
  • Water-based removers for latex and water-based sealers — these use different chemistry to break down the polymer without harsh solvents

The process follows a consistent sequence regardless of product:

  • Apply the stripper liberally to the surface and allow it to dwell for 20–30 minutes — do not let it dry out during this time
  • Agitate with a stiff-bristle brush to work the dissolved sealer free from the paver surface and out of the texture
  • Pressure wash thoroughly to remove the dissolved sealer and chemical residue
  • Assess the result — heavy sealer buildup from multiple prior applications may require a second stripping pass

Chemical stripping is effective in most cases and does not damage the paver surface when done correctly. It is also significantly less expensive than mechanical removal.

Mechanical Removal — For Extreme Cases

When chemical stripping fails to fully remove heavy sealer buildup, or when the sealer has penetrated so deeply that chemical treatment cannot reach it all, mechanical removal becomes necessary. This involves physical abrasion of the paver surface using diamond grinding equipment or shot blasting.

Mechanical removal is thorough — it removes all surface material down to bare paver — but it is more expensive, more disruptive, and requires careful technique to avoid damaging the paver surface texture. It is appropriate for surfaces with many layers of accumulated sealer, failed epoxy-type sealers, or situations where chemical stripping has been attempted and did not achieve adequate removal.

What Happens After Stripping

Freshly stripped pavers look raw. The color appears flat and the surface looks dry — often more worn and aged than expected. This is completely normal. Years of yellowed or tinted sealer have been masking the paver's actual current condition, and stripping reveals what the pavers really look like underneath.

The stripped surface also needs time before new sealer can be applied:

  • Standard drying time: 24–48 hours in Arizona summer conditions
  • After heavy stripping or wet conditions: 48–72 hours with moisture meter verification

One benefit that surprises many homeowners: stripping often reveals the original paver color beneath years of yellowed or discolored sealer buildup. The fresh surface looks dramatically better than expected — sometimes nearly as vibrant as installation day — before new sealer even goes on.

Cost of Stripping

Stripping adds cost to a sealing job, but significantly less than dealing with a failed seal application months later:

  • Chemical stripping add-on: $0.25–$0.50 per square foot on top of standard sealing pricing
  • Mechanical removal: priced by the job based on surface condition and equipment requirements
  • Full strip and reseal packages: bundled pricing available — call for a quote specific to your surface

For surfaces that clearly need stripping, doing it right the first time is always the better financial decision. A strip-and-reseal that lasts 2–3 years costs far less than resealing over a failing surface, watching it fail again in 6 months, and then stripping and resealing anyway.
